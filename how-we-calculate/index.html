How we calculate

The calculator puts every payment for each of the four options on a monthly timeline, converts it to today's money at your cost of capital, and compares the totals. Here is exactly how, and where the example figures come from.

The method in five steps

  1. Build each option's cash flows month by month over the period you choose. Month 0 is day one.
  2. Discount them to today's money using your cost of capital, turned into a monthly rate. The result is the net present value (NPV) of the cost.
  3. Rank the options by that figure. Lowest wins. If the top two are within 2% of each other, we say the result is too close to call.
  4. Turn it into a yearly and an hourly figure. The yearly equivalent cost spreads the NPV evenly over the period. Dividing by working hours, after downtime, gives the cost per working hour.
  5. Test what would change the answer. We vary hours, resale value, cost of capital, price, hire purchase rate, lease payment, pay-per-result price and maintenance, and report the three nearest points at which a different option wins.

Formulas

Net present value of the cost, where C is the cost in month t, r the annual cost of capital and N the number of months:

NPV = Σ C(t) ÷ (1 + m)^t,   where m = (1 + r)^(1/12) − 1

Yearly equivalent cost over T years:

Yearly cost = NPV × r ÷ (1 − (1 + r)^−T)

The lease interest rate is the monthly rate at which the lease payments, fees and end-of-lease amount equal the robot's price, multiplied by 12 to give an APR. If you hand the robot back, its resale value counts as part of what you pay, because the lessor keeps it.

Tax, without picking a country

Tax rules differ by country, so we ask for two numbers instead of assuming one country's rules: your tax rate, and the share of the purchase you can write off in year one. The rest is written off evenly over the remaining years. Running costs, lease payments and pay-per-result fees are deducted in the year they are paid. With hire purchase you own the robot, so it gets the same write-down as buying outright, and only the interest part of each payment is deducted. A lease gets no write-down on the robot itself. Tax relief arrives at the end of each year. Resale proceeds are taxed at the end. Grants are treated as reducing the cost and as not taxable. This is a simplification. Check the real treatment with your accountant.

Limits

  • Running costs are flat over the period. In practice maintenance tends to rise as the robot ages.
  • The lease runs for the same length as the comparison period.
  • The calculator can't put a value on flexibility, such as the option to leave a pay-per-result contract.
  • Payback uses your own labour-saving estimate and is not discounted.
