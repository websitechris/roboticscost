Robotics as a service: how it's priced and when it pays

Robotics as a service (RaaS) means you rent a robot's work instead of owning the robot. You pay a fee per month, per hour or per task, and the provider usually handles maintenance, software and repairs.

How RaaS is priced

Pricing modelHow it worksWatch for
Monthly subscriptionA fixed fee for the robot and its supportMinimum term, yearly price rises, usage caps

Per taskA fee per pick, part, or area cleanedHow a "task" is counted, and minimum volumes

Published prices are rare. Two public examples: a floor-cleaning robot rental programme announced in April 2026 started at $750 a month on one- or two-year contracts, with maintenance included (ISSA). One US integrator advertises RaaS from $499 a month (RobotLAB). Larger industrial robots cost far more.

When paying per result beats buying

  • Low or uncertain use. If the robot works one shift or less, you are paying for an owned robot to sit idle.
  • Trials. A short contract tests whether automation works for you before you commit capital.
  • Fast-moving technology. You can switch to a newer model at the end of the contract instead of being stuck with an old one.
  • No engineering team. The provider fixes faults, so downtime is their problem. An uptime guarantee puts that in writing.

When buying usually wins

  • Heavy, steady use, such as two or three shifts for several years.
  • Cheap money. If you can borrow at a low rate, owning costs less over time.
  • Good resale value, or a grant towards the purchase.

Per-hour pricing makes the trade-off easy to see. Above a certain number of hours a year, buying becomes cheaper. The calculator finds that break-even point for your figures: set the pay-per-result charge to "per hour" in the calculator.

Questions to ask a RaaS provider

  1. What exactly does the fee include: maintenance, software updates, insurance, spare parts, call-outs?
  2. How much does the price rise each year, and is that capped?
  3. What is the minimum term, and what does it cost to leave early?
  4. What uptime do you guarantee, and what do I get if you miss it?
  5. Are there usage caps or overage charges?
  6. Who owns the data the robot collects?
  7. Can I buy the robot at the end, and for how much?

RaaS and the balance sheet

A subscription is often treated as an operating cost. But under the IFRS 16 and ASC 842 lease standards, a contract that gives you control of a specific robot can count as a lease and go on the balance sheet. Ask your accountant before you rely on either treatment.
