Lease or buy? Why paying more in total can still cost you less

Our calculator sometimes says a monthly plan is cheaper than paying up front, even when the monthly payments add up to more. That looks wrong at first. Here is why it happens, with round numbers, and the one question that settles it for you.

The short answer. A monthly plan that ends with you owning the robot is really a loan. So the only question is: is the plan's interest rate lower or higher than what your own money earns, or costs you?

Lower, and the monthly plan wins. Higher, and paying up front wins. The bigger total on its own doesn't decide it.

An example with round numbers

A robot costs $20,000. The seller offers two ways to pay, and you own it at the end either way:

  • Pay now: $20,000 today.
  • Pay monthly: $400 a month for 5 years. That adds up to $24,000.

The monthly plan costs $4,000 more in total. That $4,000 is the interest on borrowing $20,000 for five years, and it works out at about 7.4% a year.

Now picture keeping the $20,000 and paying the $400 a month out of it. What happens depends on what that money does while it waits.

If your money sits in savings earning 4%

The savings grow, but not fast enough. By the end of year five you would have run out, and be about $2,100 short. Paying now is cheaper.

If your money is worth 12% a year to you

For example, it would otherwise pay off a loan or card charging 12%, or it earns that much in your business. Then the pot outgrows the payments, and you finish about $3,100 ahead. Paying monthly is cheaper, even though it adds up to $4,000 more.

"Worth today" means: the amount you would need to set aside now, earning that rate, to cover every payment. It is what our calculator calls cost in today's money, and it's why we rank on it rather than on the total.

Which rate should you use?

  • Paying from savings: use what your savings actually earn, after tax. Often 3% to 5%.
  • Paying would mean borrowing elsewhere: use that loan's rate. A card or personal loan is often far higher than the plan's rate.
  • You run a business: use your borrowing rate, or the return you expect on money kept in the business. Many firms use 8% to 12%.

If you're not sure, try two or three rates in the calculator and see whether the answer changes. If it doesn't, you have your answer.

Two more things that change the answer

Do you own it at the end, or hand it back?

Many home robots, such as 1X NEO, are offered on a monthly plan where you never own the robot. That is renting, not borrowing. Then the question becomes how long you will keep it: renting wins for a short time, buying wins if you keep it for years. Our robot maid page works out that break-even for you. Remember too that a robot you own is worth something when you sell it, and a rented one isn't.

Tax, for businesses

A business can usually claim tax relief on a robot it buys, often all in the first year, and on lease payments as it makes them. That can swing the result again. Our worked examples show the sums under each country's rules: UK, US and Australia.
